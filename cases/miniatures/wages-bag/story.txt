THE WAGES BAG

I am Samuel Orchard, clerk at Garth's Mill. At eight o'clock on Friday evening I locked the week's wages in their bag in the counting-room, hung the key on its nail in the passage, and walked down to the village for my supper. I was back at nine. The counting-room door was locked and the key was on its nail, and the bag was gone. Who took the wages bag?

Two men live at the mill besides myself: Mr. Wicks, the foreman, and Job Tarrant, the carter. I asked Tarrant on Saturday where he had been. "At the forge from eight till nine, mending a trace," he told me. "I never left it." But I had come back by the forge at ten minutes to nine, and I had looked in. It was dark, there was nobody there, and the ashes were cold to my hand.

That same morning I found the lantern from the counting-room standing in the cart-shed. I put it to Tarrant, and he changed his tale. "A little after eight I took the key off its nail and went into the counting-room for the lantern, and I hung the key back. And I will tell you more. At half past eight I saw Mr. Wicks in the counting-room." Where was Mr. Wicks at half past eight?

Mrs. Dabney cooks at the mill house, across the yard. "Mr. Wicks sat in my kitchen from eight till nine, at his supper," she told me.

Mr. Lisle, the curate, walks home from evensong by the mill lane. "The church clock struck the half hour as I passed the mill house," he told me. "At half past eight I saw Mr. Wicks through the kitchen window, sitting at the table."

The constable came on Saturday afternoon and found the wages bag under the seat of Tarrant's cart. Tarrant owned that he had taken it when he went in for the lantern, and that he had not seen Mr. Wicks at all: Mr. Wicks had been in the kitchen.
