THE LAST FERRY

I am Josiah Crane, vicar of St. Olave's at Marrow Quay. On Sunday, when evensong was over, I set the silver christening cup back on its shelf in the vestry and walked home. At nine o'clock I came back for my spectacles, unlocked the vestry door, and found the shelf bare. The lock had not been forced.

My first thought was of Silas Bell, the carter, who had quarrelled with me over the pew rents. As I left after evensong I had seen him go out at the lych-gate with a sack over his shoulder, and the lamp at the gate was not yet lit. If the cup went in that sack, it went before the lamp was lit. So was it before the lamp was lit, or after?

Tam Reed lights the gate lamp every evening, and I knocked at his door that night. "When I lit the lamp," he said, "the vestry window was bright, and I could see the cup on its shelf. Amos Dunn the sexton was at the vestry door with his keys. I saw him lock it and go off down the lane."

Bell lives across the river, and Old Hobb's ferry is the only boat on this water. On Monday morning I was at the landing when it came back. "Bell came aboard with his sack as I cast off," said Hobb. "We were in mid-river when I saw the gate lamp kindled behind us. I landed him on the far bank as the clock struck eight, and the boat lay there all night."

There are two keys to the vestry. Mine had been in my pocket all the evening. The other is the sexton's.

I went to Amos Dunn's cottage. He brought the cup out from under the coke in the boiler-house, and wept. He had gone back with his key when Tam Reed was out of sight, he said, meaning only to pawn it until harvest.
