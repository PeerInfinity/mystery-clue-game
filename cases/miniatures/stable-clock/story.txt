THE STABLE CLOCK

I am Arthur Penrose, and in October I was staying at Coldharbour Hall with my old friend Sir Giles Royle. On the Thursday evening I sat in the library with his nephew, Mr. Edwin Royle, over a game of chess. He left me at ten minutes to nine by the hall clock, saying he had a letter to write.

A little later a shot was fired through the window of Sir Giles's study. Sir Giles had gone up to bed early, and the bullet was found in the panelling above the chair where he sits of an evening. When was the shot fired?

Susan, the housemaid, was on the back stairs. "It came just as the hall clock was striking nine," she told me. "I counted the strokes."

Bowker, the gardener, was at the lodge. "It was a quarter to nine," he told me. "I looked out at the stable clock the moment I heard it." If Bowker was right, Mr. Royle was still at the chessboard with me when the shot was fired.

I had set the hall clock myself that morning, by the church clock in the village, for it had stopped in the night.

On Friday morning I walked across to the stable yard with my watch, which I had set by the hall clock. When my watch said ten, the stable clock stood at a quarter to ten.

The inspector put the times to Mr. Royle that afternoon, and he owned to it. He had gone out by the garden door with his uncle's revolver, and fired at nine.
