THE SAILOR'S KNOT

I am John Hale, twenty years at sea, and this autumn I am staying with my sister, Mrs. Ashby, at Fernleigh. On Thursday the silver plate was on the dining-room sideboard when the table was cleared at nine. At half past ten, walking in the garden with my pipe, I found it in the shrubbery, bundled up in the dining-room tablecloth. Who tied the bundle?

It was tied with a carrick bend, drawn up hard. I know the knot as I know my own hand: it is a seaman's knot, for joining two hawsers, and a landsman does not tie it.

Every eye in the house went at once to Tom Pike, the cook's brother, an able seaman home on leave, who had spent the afternoon in the kitchen with his sister. Our groom, Sam Rudd, and Ellen, the parlourmaid, were at Fernleigh all evening.

The landlord of the Bell told me that Pike sat in his taproom from seven until he closed at eleven, and never stirred.

In Rudd's box I found his discharge book: four years in the Grimsby fishing fleet as a boy. Every lad in that fleet learns the carrick bend.

I asked Ellen to tie up a parcel for the post in front of me. She tied a granny knot, and owned that she knew no other.

When I laid the bundle in front of Rudd, he owned that he had tied it, and meant to carry it off in the night.
