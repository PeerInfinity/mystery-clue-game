Silver Blaze how to play

← Back to the game

You read a Sherlock Holmes story a paragraph at a time and try to work things out before Holmes does.

This is an experiment, and it is unfinished. The game tries to turn a detective's reasoning into moves you can make: noticing, connecting, doubting, ruling out. Some parts are rough, and some screens show more machinery than a finished game would.

This guide gives nothing away. Its examples come from the story's opening, where you learn that a famous racehorse has disappeared and its trainer has been found dead, or they are made up.

1. What the game is

The game is built on Silver Blaze, a Sherlock Holmes story by Arthur Conan Doyle, first published in 1892. You read it a paragraph at a time. Holmes reasons his way through it, and you try to make the same steps before the story shows him making them.

Each step is a move: you might connect two clues into a conclusion, rule out an answer, notice that something is odd, or plan a check. The game tests your move against the clues you hold. It never tells you whether the move is the one Holmes will make. You learn that only when the story reaches the point where he makes it.

The story never changes. You can read it to the end without making a single move, but then Holmes gets everywhere first.

2. Finding your way round the screen

  • The header holds the title, a progress line (cards held, questions settled, and Ahead of Holmes: N), the ⚙ Settings menu, the ✎ Notes switch, and ?, which opens this guide (on a phone, the guide is in Settings).
  • The reading bar sits under the header. It has Next paragraph and the other reading buttons, the Hint button, how far you have read, and short messages from the game.
  • Two panels fill the rest of the screen. Each panel has a row of tabs along its top, such as Story ¶12 Card Cards Questions, and either panel can show any tab. Choose a tab in one panel that the other panel is showing, and the two panels swap.

When you click a link, such as a card's name, it opens in the other panel, so what you were looking at stays in view. If it opened in place, a ← back to … link appears under the tabs and takes you back.

When nothing is open in the Card tab, it shows a short "How to play" reminder that changes with the difficulty.

3. Reading and collecting cards

Reading on

Press Next paragraph to read one more paragraph. The newest paragraph is marked with a bar in the margin, and every paragraph has its number in the margin. Hints and the record refer to paragraphs by these numbers. Two more buttons appear when they would save you clicks:

  • Next scene reads on to the start of the next scene.
  • Next deduction reads on to just before Holmes next works something out, so you get one last try before he does. The button shows how many paragraphs it will read. Near the end it becomes To the end.

After each read, the reading bar says what it brought, for example "¶4 read · 3 new cards". A small pop-up names the new cards.

Collecting cards

Every person, animal, place, thing, document, event and statement the story mentions becomes a card. How you get cards depends on the difficulty:

  • Trivial, Easy and Medium: cards are dealt. Each one arrives when the story first mentions it. The words that mention a card are marked in the text, and clicking them opens the card.
  • Hard: cards are collected. Nothing in the text is marked. To get a card, click the words that mention it. If they mention something, the card is yours and the words get a faint green dotted underline. If they mention nothing, the game says "Nothing to note there". Clicking words you have already collected opens their card.
How the marks look on Easy and Medium (a made-up sentence):
The cook said the cake was on the pantry shelf at noon, and the cake vanished before tea.
Solid underline: a person, place or thing. Shaded: a statement (a clue). Dotted: an event or a question. Dashed: a time. On Hard, only words you have collected are marked: like this.

When the same words mention several cards, a small menu lets you choose one.

4. Cards

A card opens in the Card tab. From top to bottom it shows:

  • its kind (person, place, clue, event, inference, background…) and any flags, such as marked odd or suspected, plus other names the story has used for it;
  • what the story has said about it, grouped by paragraph, each part quoted. Anything you have not seen yet is tagged new. Click a paragraph number to jump to that place in the story;
  • under Words, explanations of old or unusual words the story uses. Under Background, general knowledge a reader of the time would have had (see Background facts);
  • the moves you can make with this card. Each one is described in the sections below;
  • the card's own Timeline, where you can pencil in where it was and when;
  • the open questions on the card, each with its possible answers;
  • for a person or a thing, Where it fits: the questions on other cards it answers, or might.

The Cards tab

Cards lists every card you hold. You can search it by name and filter it by kind, or by New (never opened) and Updated (the story has added something since you last looked). The tab's label counts the new and updated cards.

Setting a card aside

Some cards are scenery. Set aside takes a card out of your lists without losing it. Set-aside cards are kept at the bottom of the Cards tab, each with a Put back button.

“What do I know about this?”

On Medium and Hard, background knowledge comes when you ask. Press What do I know about this? on a card, and any facts you recall about it are added to that card and to the Background tab. More may come to mind about the same card later in the story.

5. Questions and their answers

Cards carry open questions, such as who did something, where, when, whose a thing is, or whether a statement is true. The Questions tab lists the questions the case turns on, as the story raises them.

Each question shows its possible answers as chips, like pencil marks in a sudoku. Click a chip for its menu. A made-up example:

Who took the cake?
the cook the gardener the butler … 1 your clues already rule out
crossed out: the cat
ruled out, with evidence: the vicar ✓
  • Circle the answers you favour (heavy outline). Circling is just for you and proves nothing.
  • A crossed-out answer is struck through in pencil. An answer ruled out with evidence is struck through on green, because the game checked it.
  • On Easy, answers your clues already exclude are greyed and folded away under a "show" link.
  • When one answer is left, or you prove one outright, the question is settled and the answer shows solid green: the cook.
  • "3 so far" instead of "3 left" means the story may still bring in an answer you have not met yet.

Proposing answers (Hard)

On Hard the list starts empty. Type an answer into + propose an answer, or pick one from or choose…. The game checks only that it is the right kind of thing (a person for "who", a place for "where"), not whether it is right. Take back removes an answer you proposed.

6. Ruling out and proving

Click a chip to open its menu. What it offers depends on the difficulty:

Cross out (Easy)
A plain pencil mark. You can cross out anything, and Restore it later.
Rule it out (Medium and Hard)
The game checks the answer against every clue and inference you hold and trust. It is ruled out only if they really exclude it. If they do not, you get "Not yet: nothing you hold and trust rules it out", and the usual cure is to build the inference that would.
Prove it's this
The same check, but it must leave this answer as the only one. If it does, the question is settled.
Could it fit? (Easy)
Asks whether this answer could still fit everything you trust. If it cannot, the game shows the chain of clues that rules it out, and offers to doubt any of them.
Drop it…
Lets go of an answer for a reason that is not a proof. See wrong turns.

Every proof cites its reasons. A message says what a ruling-out rests on ("It rests on 3 of your clues and inferences"), and on Trivial each excluded answer has a Why not?. If you later doubt a clue that a proof rested on, the proof is flagged: "A proof here rested on …, which you now doubt."

If the clues you trust contradict each other, a question can be left with nothing that fits. The game says so and offers Find the clash.

7. Inferences: connecting cards

An inference is a step of reasoning that connects clues, such as "The cake was on the shelf at noon and gone by tea, so it was taken in the afternoon" (a made-up example). Once you hold an inference, it counts like a clue you trust. It is often what lets you rule an answer out.

Building one

  1. Open a clue, an event, a background fact or another inference, and press Infer from this.
  2. A Building an inference bar appears above the card. Open another card and press Connect to this.
  3. The game answers each time: "You're onto something: connect one more card", "… does not connect with the others", or "You inferred: …". It may also say you need knowledge you do not have yet, in which case ask what you know about the things involved.
  4. On Hard you must also say what the conclusion is about: who, when, where, what or with what, why, or whether it is so.

Press Done to put the connection away. ✕ takes a single card out of it.

Stating it yourself

While cards are connected, the bar also offers Or state it yourself. Pick a question, choose is or is not, pick an answer, and press Does it follow?. The game tells you whether your connected cards really imply it, and never tells you what does follow.

The Inferences tab

Inferences lists every inference you hold: the ones you built, the ones a character draws aloud in the story, and on Trivial the ones the game drew for you. Each says who drew it and where, and gives a plain reading of what it means for the case. On Easy, the tab also counts how many more you could build from what you hold.

“Unless…”

Some steps lean on a general rule that can fail. Such an inference shows Unless… links, each a way the rule might fail here. Click one to raise it. If what you hold shows the exception is true, the step no longer counts. If nothing settles it, it stays as a reservation and the step still counts. If what you hold rules it out, the game says so.

8. Doubting what you are told

Until you say otherwise, the game takes every statement at its word. Witnesses can be wrong, though, and people lie. On a clue or an inference:

  • I suspect this may be false stops it counting as fact anywhere, and opens the question "Is it true?". Where it applies, you can say how it is false: the speaker was mistaken or lied. You can also record why, for instance that they could not have seen it, or that it is hearsay.
  • Trust it again undoes the doubt.
  • Split this statement appears on statements that mix what someone saw with what they concluded. It splits the statement into its parts, so you can doubt one and keep the other.
  • Passed on: shows who told whom, for a statement that came second-hand.

Clashes. When clues you trust cannot all be true, the game sets aside the one that weighs least when it checks your answers, until you doubt one yourself. Someone else's reasoning gives way first, then a rule of thumb, then a person's word, and your own reasoning last. On Easy the clashes are listed at the top of the Questions tab, each laid out step by step, with a "doubt this instead" link on each claim.

9. Odd details, absences and your own questions

“That's odd”

A good detective notices when something goes against what one would expect. On a clue, a thing or an event, press That's odd. You can choose a background fact as the reason (because…). The game tells you nothing back. It notes where you noticed, and the record compares that with Holmes later. Not odd after all takes it back.

What did not happen

Sometimes the clue is something that should have happened and did not. You can file an absence from a clue card (Should this have shown something that did not happen?) or from the Timeline's board. Choose what you would expect, when, and who or what would have noticed, then press File it. A filed absence becomes a clue of its own.

Asking your own question

On a person, thing or event card, Ask your own question offers fixed wordings, such as "Where is …?", "Whose is …?", "Why would … do it?" or "Where was … during …?". Choose one and press Ask. Your question can collect candidate answers from the cards you hold. The game checks nothing on it. If the story later asks the same question, yours joins it, and the record notes that you asked it first. This is never scored, because a question can be asked of anything.

All three appear in the Questions tab under Your questions and Odd things.

10. Theories and wrong turns

A theory is a set of answers held together: one possible account of what happened. In Theories, type a name under New theory and press Create. New answers go into whichever theory is marked ★ new answers go here. To add an answer, click its chip on any question and choose Add to “…”.

Characters in the story hold theories of their own, and these appear here as the story reaches them.

The game weighs each theory against what you trust:

  • which things you trust it needs to be wrong, and whether it goes against your own reasoning or against what was seen;
  • which of the odd things you have noticed it leaves unexplained;
  • which of its answers rest on nothing you hold.

How much it says depends on the difficulty. On Easy it names everything. On Medium it gives counts only. On Hard it says nothing until you press Weigh it.

Dropping a wrong turn

Not every wrong idea can be disproved outright. Drop it… works on a theory or on a single answer. You choose a reason: another theory explains more, it needs more people than another theory, it needs too much of what I trust to be wrong, or a card tells against it (and which card). The game checks whether your reason holds. On Easy it says why; on Medium it says only whether. A dropped answer is struck through with dots, and take it back restores it.

The board's “nights”

Each theory can also be chosen as a layer on the Timeline's board, to see where its answers put everyone, and Does this night fit? checks it (see Timeline).

11. Checks

Detectives test their ideas: they look for something, ask someone, show a thing to a witness, or compare two things. In Checks you can plan such a test before Holmes makes it.

  1. Pick a verb (Look for … at …, Ask … about …, Show … to …, Compare … with …) and fill it in with cards you hold.
  2. If you like, say what you expect (found / not found, yes / no, they match / they differ), which answer that would give, and which of your theories it comes from.
  3. Press Seal it.

A sealed check says nothing until the story gets there. Then it shows whether Holmes made the same check and, if the story tells, what the result was. A check you planned before Holmes made it counts, whatever the result. A wrong guess costs nothing. You can withdraw a check until Holmes acts on it.

12. The other tabs

Story ¶n
The text as far as you have read. The label shows the paragraph you are on.
Questions
The case's questions, with how many answers are left and which you circled. Below them: statements you doubt, your own questions, odd things, and, depending on the difficulty, the clashes and Holmes's interest (places where the story shows what Holmes pays attention to; it points, it proves nothing). As you read, a list of what the story held back also appears: facts told later than they happened, and background the game supplied.
Timeline
Five views:
  • By place and By thing: a board of where everyone and everything was, and when. Your clues place some of it (solid). The rest you pencil in yourself (dashed). Several marks in one place are several guesses, and clicking a mark strikes it. On a mark you can also prove it or rule it out. You choose whom to follow, and you can split a stretch of time at an event, into before and after.
  • What happened: the events you hold, in order, once their time is settled.
  • What you found: your cards in the order you collected them.
  • What the story told: the scenes, and how many of your clues each one first told.
Background
The background facts you have, grouped by subject. Most do not matter to the case and some do. Facts that a reader like you would not know are marked Supplied.
Notes, Hints, Record
See Notes, Hints and the record.

13. Deadlines and your record against Holmes

Each step Holmes takes has a deadline: the paragraph where the story shows him taking it. If you made the same move before that paragraph, you were ahead of him.

The game keeps quiet about steps whose deadline you have not reached. Telling you early would give away which of your moves are right. So the record grows only as you read: when you pass a deadline, a line appears in the reading bar. It might say "Holmes reasons: … You had it at paragraph 9: 4 paragraphs before him", or, if you missed it, where you could have had it and from what.

The header shows Ahead of Holmes: N, and the Record (N ahead) tab has the details. The rows are grouped under:

  • Deductions: steps of reasoning Holmes makes;
  • Odd details: things Holmes draws attention to;
  • Steps seen through: reasoning Holmes shows does not hold;
  • Wrong turns dropped: ideas the story leaves behind;
  • Checks planned: tests Holmes makes;
  • What did not happen: absences Holmes points to.

When the story ends, the record shows your best leads (Furthest ahead), every move made before Holmes, moves that did not count and why, Where you could have been first, and the questions you asked before the story did.

14. Scoring

  • You score one point for each of Holmes's steps that you made before its deadline: an inference built or an answer settled, an odd detail marked, an exception raised, a wrong turn dropped, a check sealed, or an absence filed.
  • Hearing someone in the story state an inference gives you the inference, but no point.
  • A dropped wrong turn scores only if the reason you gave actually holds.
  • A check scores whatever its result. Sealing the same check twice with opposite expectations, to cover both outcomes, does not count.
  • Your own questions are recorded but never scored.
  • Nothing is scored on Trivial, because the game does the reasoning there.
  • Hints do not cost points. A move made after a hint, or by Do it, counts like any other. The record does show how many of your moves came with hints, and how far up the hint ladder each one went.

Pace

Free
Read on whenever you like. When Holmes is about to reason, the bar just says "Holmes is a step ahead of you here".
Wait for deductions

This is the closest thing to solving the case alongside him.

16. Hints

Stuck? Press Hint in the reading bar. It points you toward the next thing you could still do before Holmes, the one whose deadline is nearest. A hint comes in four rungs, each saying a little more:

  1. Where. Which paragraphs to look at again. Click a paragraph number to jump there.
  2. What kind of move. For example, an inference to build (and what its conclusion is about), something to mark as odd, an answer to drop, or a check to plan.
  3. Which. The cards involved, or the words to click to get them.
  4. The step. The moves themselves, spelled out, with a Do it button that makes them for you on the page. You watch the game do the clicks.

Press More for the next rung. The Hint button shows how far you have gone, for example Hint (2/4). When the target is made, the bar says "Made. Press Hint for the next." If nothing you have read yet can put you ahead, it says "Read on". Once you read on, the hint folds up, and a show link opens it again.

The cooldown. After each press of Hint or More, both buttons wait a while before you can press again. They count down the seconds. The wait is 30 seconds unless you change it under Hint cooldown in Settings, and 0 turns it off.

The Hints tab lists every hint you have been given: where, what kind, how many rungs, and whether you then made the move before Holmes or he got there first.

Hints work on Easy, Medium and Hard. They do not cost points; see Scoring.

17. Your notes

You can write in the margins, so to speak. Press ✎ Notes in the header to turn on notes mode. While it is on, clicking in the story does not open or collect cards:

  • Click anywhere in the text to start a note at that point. Type it, then press Enter to keep it.
  • Drag over some words to mark them. The marking snaps to whole words and stops at the end of the paragraph. Type a note about them, or press Enter with nothing typed to keep just the highlight.
It was the favourite for the Wessex Cup front-page news that had disappeared.
A made-up note on the story's opening: a highlight and a handwritten note.

Click a note in the story to edit it. While you are writing or editing, the reading bar shows the keys and a Delete button. A deleted note can be brought back with undo. The Notes tab lists every note, with show, edit and delete.

Notes are only for you. The game never reads them, and they never affect your score. Press ✎ Notes: on again to go back to normal clicking.

18. Settings and layout

⚙ Settings has two parts.

The game

Difficulty, pace, (on Trivial) when answers settle, and the difficulty options. All of these are described above.

The page

These settings are yours. They are kept on this device and survive Start over.

Layout
Two columns (two panels side by side) or One column. A narrow screen, such as a phone, always shows one column.
Theme
As the system, Light or Dark. This guide follows the same choice.
Notes in the story
Shown: written into the text. Only in the Notes tab: a small ✎ marks each note's place, and the marked words stay highlighted.
Notes written in
Handwriting, or the page's own face.
Highlights
A yellow marker, a blue underline, or a pale blue wash.
Hint cooldown
Seconds to wait between hint presses (0 for none).

Saving and starting over

Your game saves itself in this browser as you play. Close the page and come back later, and you are where you left off. Start over… at the bottom of Settings asks first, then clears your place, cards, marks, theories, notes and hints. It keeps your page settings.

19. Watching the autoplay

Watch the autoplay… in Settings plays the whole game on Hard, through the page, while you watch a cursor make the moves. It contains every answer, so watch it only once you have played, or if you do not mind spoilers.

Your own game is put aside and comes back when you leave the autoplay. A strip at the bottom of the page (at the top on a phone) controls it:

  • Before it starts, choose what to watch: The careful reader, who makes every move a careful reader would and writes their thoughts into the story as notes (about an hour at normal speed), or The fewest moves that reach the best score, with no notes and a count of the clicks.
  • Resume / Pause. While it is paused, the page is yours to look round.
  • Step makes one move, then pauses again.
  • A speed menu runs from ¼× to 4×. The careful reader also has notes at once, which writes each note in a single go.
  • Skip to the next paragraph makes the current paragraph's moves without pausing.
  • Stop ends it. At the end, Back to your game returns you to where you were.

20. The logic viewer

A separate page, logic.html, lays the whole case open: every clue, inference, background fact and question, with how they connect, in several views you can filter and click through.

It shows every answer. It is meant for the curious after playing, and for anyone working on the game. It is not part of play, and the game itself does not link to it.

21. Mouse and keyboard tips

  • Click any answer chip for its menu. Click it again to close the menu.
  • Card names in blue are links. They open in the other panel, so you can keep the story in view.
  • A paragraph number on a card or in a hint jumps the story to that paragraph.
  • Hover over almost any setting or button for a short explanation.
  • Writing a note: Enter keeps it, Shift+Enter starts a new line, and Esc cancels. Clicking somewhere else also keeps it.
  • Naming a new theory: Enter creates it.
  • Proposing an answer on Hard: type the name and the box suggests matches, or use the or choose… menu instead.
  • Clicking anywhere outside the Settings menu closes it.
  • Sometimes a check takes a moment. A "Working it out…" box shows how far it has got, and your clicks wait until it is done.
