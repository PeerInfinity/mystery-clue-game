Watson's timeline how to play

← Back to the timeline

You read a Sherlock Holmes story, told by Dr Watson, and keep an ordered list of what happened: who was where, who did what, and when.

This guide gives nothing away. Its examples come from the story's first pages, where you learn that a famous racehorse has disappeared and its trainer has been found dead, or they are made up.

1. What it is

The page is built on Sherlock Holmes stories by Arthur Conan Doyle: Silver Blaze and The Adventure of the Speckled Band, both first published in 1892. Both cases are experimental. Watson tells each story, and you read it a paragraph at a time: what he sees over Holmes's shoulder, reads in the papers, hears from the people he meets.

New to the page? Start with one of the two short cases, invented stories of a few paragraphs that take about two minutes each and turn on one move of the game: The Lamp-Keeper's Word and The Rent Box. Each has its own narrator in Watson's place.

Everything the story tells goes onto the timeline, an ordered list of events. What Watson sees for himself is certain. Everything else reached him through someone: a witness, the police, a newspaper, Holmes. You decide how far to trust each of them, and the page shows the contradictions among what you accept.

The story raises questions as it goes (who did it? where was he? what was it for?). You answer them the way you fill in a sudoku: circle the answer you think is right, cross out the ones you have ruled out. You can also reason from what is on the timeline: point out that something must have changed, what an action needed, what it was for. The page checks each step against the timeline.

There is no score. Before the story gives an answer away you must commit to one, and where the story has given you the evidence for it, you must show the answer first: link the evidence and circle the answer it leaves.

2. The screen

  • The header holds the title, the reading controls (Read to ¶ and Read on ▶), Undo, Hint, ⚙ Settings, and a link to this guide.
  • The story: everything read so far, the newest paragraph last.
  • The timeline, with the contradictions, the questions and the cards above it.
  • The detail: whatever you last clicked, an event, a question or a name, with all there is to say about it.

On a wide screen these sit in three columns. In Settings you can choose panels with tabs instead, two side by side or one (a phone always shows one): Story Timeline Questions Contradictions Cards Detail. A link opens its tab in the other panel, so what you were looking at stays in view; in one column it opens in place, with a ← Back to … link above it.

A paragraph number, such as ¶12, anywhere on the page scrolls the story to that paragraph.

3. Reading on, and the gate

Read on ▶ reads one more paragraph. To read further at once, type a later paragraph's number in Read to ¶. Reading only goes forward: to look again at something already read, scroll up in the story, or click a paragraph number.

The gate

Sometimes the story is about to give away the answer to a question you could have worked out already. Then reading stops just before that paragraph, and a notice says which questions it answers: commit first by circling exactly one answer to each.

Where a question has evidence to link (see observations), the story goes on only once you have shown its answer: every piece of its evidence linked to it, and the right answer circled. The notice says how much of the evidence you have linked; when all of it is linked and the story still waits, the answer you circled is not the one it leads to. A statement you have decided is false counts as evidence too: link it to the question once you have distrusted it. For a question with no evidence to link, any one answer will do, right or wrong. If you would rather never be held up, Settings has To read past an answer: commit to any answer.

The notice links to each question and has its own Hint and Hide (Read on shows it again).

When you read past it, a notice says what the story has answered, what you circled, and whether you were right. The question itself keeps the answer, with the words that gave it away.

4. The timeline

Each event is one line: who, what, and where, under a heading for its point in time ("Monday evening", "Monday, nine o'clock"), with the paragraph it came from. A dot in front shows how far you trust it (see Trust). Click an event for its detail: the words of the story it comes from, when it happened, and how it reached you.

The bar above the timeline changes how it is shown:

View
List, the events in order; Grid by place, a column for each place (with Places: by region, by area, by place or in full); or Grid by source, a column for each person the news came from. In a grid, each entry starts a line below the start of the one before it, so the order in time still reads top to bottom.
Show
Everything, or Only entries with moves ⚙: the events you can reason from (see the moves).

Some lines are not the story's but yours: your circled answers (your hypotheses) and the conclusions of your reasoning (your reasoning), each at its point in time.

5. Trust: how it reached you

Every event came to you along a chain: someone saw it, told someone else, who told the papers, which Watson read, and Watson tells you. The detail of an event shows each of its chains, link by link, up to Watson, for example:

the stable lad → told → the police → told → the newspapers → printed → Watson

Each link has its own setting, and so does the statement itself (This statement itself:), apart from the rest that came the same way. There are four levels:

  • certain: Watson saw it himself. Only his own eyes are certain. (Watson is a careful narrator: what he tells you he saw, you can take as certain. Settings has Trust in Watson as narrator if you would rather not.)
  • trusted: you believe this link.
  • uncertain: you have not decided. Every link starts here.
  • distrusted: you do not believe it.

A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and an event is as trusted as its best chain. Under People, the detail also lets you cap a person: "at most uncertain" holds for everything that passed through them.

What is on the timeline depends on your trust: by default, everything at least uncertain is shown. In Settings, On the timeline from raises that bar (only what you trust), and Show the rest, faded keeps what falls below it in view, greyed. Reset trust… in Settings puts every link and person back to uncertain.

6. Contradictions and likely clashes

When two things on the timeline cannot both be true (someone in two places at once, a door both locked and open at the same moment), the page lists them as a contradiction. You never have to look for these: they appear as soon as both sides are on the timeline.

Each contradiction names its two sides; trace shows both sides' chains. A link or a person marked there is one whose distrust alone would take that side off the timeline, and the page says how many other events would go with it. To resolve a contradiction, decide which side you believe and distrust what brought the other, or change the circle that caused it.

A likely clash (in amber, unlikely together) is weaker: one of your circled answers and something on the timeline can both be true, but they sit badly together. You can I hold both if you still believe them, and Reconsider later. When the story answers a question, its detail lists what a careful reader would have noticed along the way.

7. Questions and your marks

The story raises questions as it goes. They are in the Questions list, and each also sits on the timeline at its point in time. Each question lists possible answers:

? Where is the horse now?
on the moor in its stable at another stable More…

The answers shown first are those someone in the story puts forward, and "something else". More has the rest: other ideas a reader might have and, for a who or where question, every person, animal or place met so far. Click an answer to circle it, again to cross it out, and again to leave it uncertain.

A circled answer goes on the timeline as your hypothesis, and takes part in contradictions like anything else: if your answer cannot be true alongside what you trust, you will see it. A crossed-out answer is your claim that it was not so. Any answer that fits is accepted when the story answers; there is not only one route to it.

Click a question's name for its detail: the words that raised it, when it is about, your marks and what each takes for granted, and, once the story has answered, the answer.

8. Reasoning: the moves

An event marked ⚙ on the timeline is one you can reason from. Open it, and its detail offers the moves that fit it. Each move puts a new entry on the timeline, marked as your reasoning, trusted as far as its weakest premise. The page checks every move against the timeline, and offers only those that hold.

(a) It changed
On a state (where something is, who has it, whether a door is open): a state lasts until something changes it. If the timeline has two different states of the same thing, It changed points out that something changed it between them, and that becomes a question of its own: what did?
(b) What it needs and what it does
On an action: Point out what it needs and what it does lists what had to be so just before it (to take something, you must be where it is) and what is so just after, each with what the timeline says about it.
(c) What is this for?
On an action or an intention: the intentions on the timeline it could serve, stated in the story or circled by you. For this links them; the page checks that what it brings about meets the goal, or a need of a step already linked.
(d) What would this need?
On a plan, one the story describes or one you have built by linking steps to an intention: each step's needs, met by an earlier step, by the timeline, or by nothing, and whether the steps reach the goal. See Plans.

Your reasoning rests on what it was drawn from. If that changes (you distrust a premise, or change a circle), the move stops holding: a notice in the corner says so, and the entry's detail lists it under No longer fits. It comes back if what changed changes back. Withdraw takes a move back for good.

9. Plans and their checks

Once a plan's needs are open (What would this need?), its detail offers three checks:

  • What stopped it? If an intended step never happened, what on the timeline prevented it.
  • Compare with another plan: two plans side by side, step by step, with their goals and whether each is reached.
  • Does it account for this? Whether the plan explains something else on the timeline.

A plan's needs that nothing meets are listed in red. That is often where the next question lies.

The room

Some stories turn on how a place is arranged. Those have a Room panel; the others do not.

  • The ways in. A row for each way into a closed place, as the story tells of it. Mark each one closed or open, separately for a person and for something small. The page refuses a mark nothing on the timeline supports.
  • Something got through. If something crossed between two places, there is a way between them, even one nobody has found.
  • A route. Build a way from one place to another, a link at a time, from what is on the timeline.

Observations

In stories that have it, you can point out that something on the timeline is relevant to an open question: open the event and choose under Relevant to…, or press Link observations… on a question and go down the timeline. If it tells something about that question, what it shows is written under the question, with where it came from, and it stays only as long as you trust the event. If not, your link stays as a note. Some answers are not a name but a description you build this way.

Relevant to… lists the open questions in the order of the Questions list. Put them in the order you like there: drag a question by its ≡ (or focus it and use the arrow keys), or press ↑ to send it to the top. A dividing line in that list decides what is offered: drag it up and only the questions above it appear, with Show the others for the rest. A question newly raised comes in just above the line.

10. Cards and the story's names

Every name in the story is a link: a person, a horse, a place, a thing. Click one for its card in the detail: the questions about it, the sentences of the story that mention it, and its own timeline (only the events it takes part in).

The Cards list holds everyone and everything met so far, by kind (people, animals, places, things…), each a link to its card.

11. Hints

Hint says what a careful reader would do next, from wherever you are. If one of your marks is wrong where it matters, or you distrust something a careful reader would believe, the hint starts there. Otherwise it points to the next step: a question to answer before the story gives it away, a move to make, or simply "read on".

Each press goes a step further: first where to look, then the reasoning, then the move itself, with Do it to make it for you. After each press the button waits a little (30 seconds unless you change it in Settings), so that you think before you ask again.

12. Undo

Undo takes back your last change of trust, of a mark, or of your reasoning, one at a time. Reading on is not undone.

13. Watching the autoplay

Watch the autoplay… in Settings shows a careful reader filling in the timeline through the page, from the first paragraph to the last: every circle, every trust, every move, with a caption saying what each one is and why. It contains every answer, so watch it only once you have played, or if you do not mind spoilers.

Your own timeline is kept aside and comes back when you stop. A strip of controls goes with it: it starts paused: choose a speed, then Start; then Pause / Resume, Step (the next move or paragraph, then pause), the speed, from ¼× to 4×, and Stop. At normal speed it takes about a quarter of an hour.

14. Settings and starting over

⚙ Settings has two parts. Clicking anywhere outside it closes it.

The timeline

On the timeline from
How far you must trust an event for it to be on the timeline (see Trust).
Show the rest, faded
Keep what falls below that in view, greyed.
Trust in Watson as narrator
How far you trust what Watson tells you. Everything in the story comes to you through him, so lowering it lowers everything; by default it is certain.

The page

Layout
Three columns, Two columns, with tabs or One column, with tabs. A phone always shows one.
Theme
As the system, Light or Dark. This guide follows the same choice.
Wait between hints
None, 10 or 30 seconds, or a minute.
To read past an answer
What the gate asks of a question that has evidence to link: show it (the evidence linked and the right answer circled; the default), or commit to any answer.

Saving and starting over

Your timeline saves itself in this browser as you play: close the page and come back, and you are where you left off. Reset trust… puts every link and person back to uncertain (and Watson as narrator back to certain) and keeps the rest. Start over… asks first, then clears everything (your place in the story, your trust, marks and reasoning) and starts again from the first paragraph. It keeps the layout, the theme, the wait between hints and the gate's rule.

Hover over almost any button or setting for a short explanation.

15. The earlier game

Watson's timeline grew out of an earlier game on Silver Blaze, in which you collect cards and build inferences to beat Holmes to his deductions. It is kept as it was, with its own guide: the earlier game (also in Settings, The earlier game…). The two keep separate saves.
