The timeline how to play

← Back to the case

You read a detective story a paragraph at a time, and keep an ordered list of what happened: who was where, who did what, and when.

This guide gives nothing away. Its examples are made up: a small affair at a country vicarage, told by the curate, in which the vicar's silver inkstand has gone from his study. No case is about it.

1. What it is

The page plays short cases: invented stories of a few paragraphs, about two minutes each, each turning on one move of the game. The start page lists them; start anywhere.

Every case is told by a narrator: someone in the story who tells you what they saw, heard and read. The header names whose timeline it is. Some cases have two narrators, each telling part of it.

Everything the story tells goes onto the timeline, an ordered list of events. What the narrator sees for themselves is confirmed. Everything else reached the narrator through someone: a witness, a constable, a letter, a neighbour. It starts as uncertain, shown in grey, and counts for nothing until you decide how far to trust it. The page shows the contradictions among what you accept.

The story raises questions as it goes (who took it? where was she? when did it happen?). You answer them the way you fill in a sudoku, by ruling answers out. You do it by linking observations, the events on the timeline that bear on a question: the page crosses out the answers that what you have linked rules out, and circles the one that follows. (A setting lets you circle and cross out the answers yourself instead; some questions are always marked that way.) You can also reason from what is on the timeline: point out that something must have changed, or what an action needed. The page checks each step against the timeline.

There is no score. Before the story gives an answer away you must show it: link the evidence that leaves one answer standing.

2. The screen

  • The header holds the case's title, the reading controls (Read to ¶ and Read on ▶), Undo, Hint, ⚙ Settings, and a link to this guide.
  • The story: everything read so far, the newest paragraph last.
  • The timeline, with the contradictions, the questions, the sources and the cards above it.
  • The detail: whatever you last clicked, an event, a question or a name, with all there is to say about it.

On a wide screen these sit in three columns. In Settings you can choose panels with tabs instead, two side by side or one (a phone always shows one): Story Timeline Questions Contradictions Sources Cards Detail. A link opens its tab in the other panel, so what you were looking at stays in view; in one column it opens in place, with a ← Back to … link above it.

A paragraph number, such as ¶4, anywhere on the page scrolls the story to that paragraph.

3. Reading on, and the gate

Read on ▶ reads one more paragraph. To read further at once, type a later paragraph's number in Read to ¶. Reading only goes forward: to look again at something already read, scroll up in the story, or click a paragraph number.

Papers

In some cases the story hands the narrator papers as it goes: a statement taken down, a letter, notes. Each paper gets a tab at the top of the Story panel when the story reaches it, beside The story, with how far you have read it (1/2). Read the papers in any order, and leave one half read if you like: Read on ▶ and Read to ¶ work on the tab you are looking at. A paragraph A paper you open before reading any of it shows no text yet, only a button, Read its first paragraph ▶, which does what Read on ▶ does. A paragraph of a paper is numbered within it, with the paper's short name: Statement ¶2. A paper never gives an answer away; the story itself does. Read on ▶ in the story does not read the papers for you: while a paper that has come is not read to its end, a note under the story says so, with a button to open it, and the gate's notice lists it.

In other cases papers are earned: a few are in the narrator's hands from the start, and each of the rest comes when you solve a question (see solved questions). Which question brings which paper is drawn at random for each new game, so two games of the same case can go in a different order; some questions bring nothing new. The papers, under the tabs, lists each paper you have and how it came: in hand from the start, or brought by solving which question. Some questions are also answered when you solve them: a short paper comes in which somebody in the story confirms the answer. The main story can be read from the start, all but its ending, which waits until the case's main question is solved; the other questions can be left open, and solved afterwards.

Solve the smaller questions first. The main question of such a case rests on the others, and most of its evidence is in papers you do not have at the start: each smaller question solved may bring one. While the ending is held, the notice says so and lists the questions whose evidence is all on the page now.

The gate

Sometimes the story is about to give away the answer to a question you could have worked out already. Then reading stops just before that paragraph, and a notice says which questions it answers.

The story goes on only once you have shown each answer: every piece of the question's evidence linked to it (see observations), and the right answer circled. The notice says how much of the evidence you have linked; when all of it is linked and the story still waits, the answer you circled is not the one the evidence leaves. A statement you have decided is false counts as evidence too: link it to the question once you have distrusted it (as unlikely, or as ruled out). A question with no evidence to link takes any one answer, right or wrong. If you would rather never be held up, Settings has To read past an answer: commit to any answer.

The notice links to each question and has its own Hint and Hide (Read on shows it again).

When you read past it, a notice says what the story has answered, what you circled, and whether you were right. The question itself keeps the answer, with the words that gave it away.

4. The timeline

Each event is one line: who, what, and where, under a heading for its point in time ("Sunday morning", "Sunday, during the service"), with the paragraph it came from. A dot in front shows how far you trust it (see Trust). Click an event for its detail: the words of the story it comes from, when it happened, and how it reached you.

The bar above the timeline changes how it is shown:

View
List, the events in order; Grid by place, a column for each place (with Places: by region, by area, by place or in full); or Grid by source, a column for each person the news came from. In a grid, each entry starts a line below the start of the one before it, so the order in time still reads top to bottom.
Show
Everything, or Only entries with moves ⚙: the events you can reason from (see the moves).

Some lines are not the story's but yours: your circled answers (your hypotheses) and the conclusions of your reasoning (your reasoning), each at its point in time.

5. Trust: how it reached you

Every event came to you along a chain: someone saw it, told someone else, who told the narrator, and the narrator tells you. The detail of an event shows each of its chains, link by link, up to the narrator, for example:

the sexton → told → the housekeeper → told → the curate

There are five levels of trust, and the timeline shows each in its own way (dashed means likely either way, solid means settled):

  • confirmed: it is so. It counts as evidence, and what follows from confirmed evidence can be certain. What the narrator saw for themselves starts here. You cannot set this level yourself: a statement earns it (below).
  • likely: you take it to be so. It counts as evidence, and what follows from it is likely at most.
  • grey, no dot: uncertain, you have not decided. Everything anyone but the narrator says starts here. It is shown, and it counts for nothing: it is no evidence for any question.
  • struck through, dashed: unlikely. You take it to be false; as a lie it can be likely evidence.
  • struck through, solid: ruled out. It is false; as a lie it can be certain evidence.

So to use what a witness says you trust it as likely; and when it cannot be so, you distrust it, as unlikely or as ruled out. A conclusion is only as strong as the weakest thing it rests on.

Two things move trust without a control. Linking a statement to a question as evidence trusts it as likely: to cite a thing is to hold it likely (unless you have already distrusted it). And solving a question confirms what its answer rested on: the statements you linked for it and held likely become confirmed, and the notice says how many.

Confirmed is earned. No control offers it for what someone says. A statement becomes confirmed in two ways: a question you solve rested on it, or two sources that share nothing agree on it and you take the offer (see Two sources that agree, below). Once a statement has earned it you can lower it and put it back. A narrator's own trust can always be set back to confirmed.

Where trust is set

You can set trust at three heights, and the most specific setting wins:

  • One statement: in the event's detail, This statement itself.
  • A paper, or everything someone told at one time: the link of its chain (in the detail, or on the paper's own entry: Everything it says).
  • A person: under People in the detail, or in the Sources list.

Setting a person sets everything they said to that level; setting a paper sets everything in it. After that you can still set one statement apart ("take the doctor at his word, but rule out this one sentence"). Where a statement came through two people, one telling the other, it is as trusted as the weaker of the two, unless you set the statement itself.

Sources, above the timeline, lists the narrator and everyone whose words have reached you, each with its level and a count of what they said at each level. Click a name for what came from them, one layer down: their papers or tellings, and under each of those its statements, each with its own setting.

A source's page, and their card, give their record so far: how many of their own statements are confirmed, how many stand against what is confirmed, and how many of the things that came from them you have ruled out. The same is said beside a statement of theirs in its detail and in a clash, where two accounts disagree and you have to weigh them. The record sets nothing, and it does not say which of the rest is true: someone who is wrong once is often right about the rest. It changes one thing. A link normally trusts its entry as likely; once you have ruled out something that came from a source, a link to anything else from them is kept but waits, with a button, until you trust it yourself. (If a statement was changed on the way, by whoever carried it, rule out the carrier's telling and not the statement: it is then laid to the carrier.)

Two sources that agree. Where two statements say the same thing of the same moment, and came from sources that share nothing (no person and no paper in common; what the narrator saw is apart from everyone), the Contradictions part lists them under agree. Where one of them is confirmed, or both are likely, a button beside the other offers Trust as confirmed. It is only an offer: two people can agree and both be wrong.

What an answer rests on. With the answers marked from the evidence, a question that has its answer says, under what follows, what that answer rests on by what you have linked: what the narrator saw, whose word, another question's answer. If it all comes through one person it says the answer hangs on their word alone, with their record where something of theirs is ruled out or stands against what is confirmed. If the answer follows more than one way, it says so, and whether two of the ways share no source (then it is corroborated) or all pass through one person. It speaks only of what you have linked, never of what else there may be.

The narrator is a source too. Everything in the story comes to you through the narration, and by default you take it as confirmed. Lower it (in Sources, or in Settings: Trust in … as narrator) if you think a narrator is mistaken or is not telling you the truth: nothing they pass on can then be more trusted than they are.

Next to each trust control, an event's detail says what ruling it out would stop from counting: a statement can be partly true, and ruling out the true part loses it. A statement's own entry lists what it says: that it was said stands, and what it says is trusted apart. Evidence you have linked to a question waits while you distrust it: the question and the entry's detail then say that it does not count. What is ruled out stays in view, struck through, unless you hide it (Settings: Hide what is ruled out). Reset trust… in Settings takes back every level you have set.

6. Contradictions and likely clashes

When two things on the timeline cannot both be true (someone in two places at once, a door both locked and open at the same moment, a thing said to have happened and shown not to have), the page lists them as a contradiction. You never have to look for these: they appear as soon as both sides are on the timeline.

How strong a clash is depends on its weaker side:

  • A contradiction: both sides are confirmed. One of them has to go.
  • Cannot be so: against what is confirmed: one side is confirmed and the other is not, so the other cannot stand as it is. Where that side is a statement, Distrust as ruled out is right there; where it is your own circle, change the circle.
  • Unlikely together (in amber): neither side is confirmed, and both are at least likely.
  • Cannot both be so: undecided (in grey): neither side is confirmed, and one at least is still uncertain. Nothing follows yet; it shows you where a decision is waiting.

Each side that is not confirmed has its level beside it. (Settings can leave what is still uncertain out of all this: Clashes with what is uncertain.)

Each clash names its two sides; trace shows both sides' chains, with buttons to trust or distrust each statement. A link or a person marked there is one whose ruling out alone would stop that side counting, and the page says how many other events would go with it. A clash can stay open as long as you like. To resolve one, decide which side you believe and distrust the other, or change the circle that caused it. The page never sets a level for you.

A likely clash (in amber, unlikely together) is weaker: one of your circled answers and something on the timeline can both be true, but they sit badly together (these are written by the case's author). You can I hold both if you still believe them, and Reconsider later.

7. Questions and your marks

The story raises questions as it goes. They are in the Questions list, and each also sits on the timeline at its point in time. Each question lists possible answers:

? Who took the inkstand?
the sexton the housekeeper the organist More…

The answers shown first are those someone in the story puts forward, and "something else". More has the rest: other ideas a reader might have and, for a who or where question, every person, animal or place met so far. By default the page marks them from the evidence you link: an answer is crossed out when what you have shown rules it out, and circled when it follows (Settings: Answers). With marked by you, and on a question the page cannot mark, you click an answer to circle it, again to cross it out, and again to leave it uncertain; the rest of this section is about such marks of your own.

Where a question asks who did something that could only be done on the spot (opened a door, took a thing from a room), circling a name says that they were there. If the timeline has them somewhere else for the whole of the time it could have happened, that is a contradiction like any other: take the circle off, or decide you do not believe whoever puts them elsewhere.

Some answers say something plain about the timeline: "yes" to whether a man was in a place at an hour is that man in that place at that hour. Circle such an answer and the page holds you to it; cross it out and the page holds you to the opposite, and either can contradict a statement you still believe.

A circled answer goes on the timeline as your hypothesis, and takes part in the clashes like anything else: if your answer cannot be true alongside what you trust, you will see it. A guess counts as likely, so such a clash is listed as unlikely together, or, against something confirmed, as cannot be so. Where the question asks who did something, circling someone also brings in what doing it needs (being there; knowing how): if the timeline has them elsewhere the whole time, or says they could not, that is a contradiction too, and the question's detail lists each need with what the timeline says. A crossed-out answer is your claim that it was not so. Any answer that fits is accepted when the story answers; there is not only one route to it.

Click a question's name for its detail: the words that raised it, when it is about, your marks and what each takes for granted, and, once the story has answered, the answer.

Left to try

Above the questions, Left to try counts what you have not done yet: 1 paper to read · 2 moves · 310 links · 4 answers. Open it for the lists. Reading: the story and each paper not read to its end; click one to open it. Reasoning moves on offer: every move the page would take now and that you have not made, with the entry it is made from; click the entry to open it. A move is on offer because it holds, not because the story needs it. Links not tried yet: for each open question, how many entries are neither linked to it nor tried for it; click the question to go down them on the timeline. Questions with no answer circled. Trust is not counted: any statement can always be trusted more or less, and contradictions are what tell you where to look.

Solved questions

A question with evidence to link is solved the moment all of its evidence is linked (see observations) and exactly one answer is circled, the right one. You need not wait for the story to reach it. A notice says so, and the question is marked ✓ solved. Solved is for good: its circle stays (Undo does not take it back, and nor does distrusting its evidence afterwards), the other answers put forward are crossed out, and a click on another answer only crosses it out or clears it. If all the evidence is linked and the answer you circled is wrong, the question says Not this answer., and nothing more. Until then the question says Not solved yet, with what it has so far: whether an answer is circled, and how many pieces of its evidence are in place, or that some of its evidence is not on the page yet (a paper that has not come, a paragraph not read), and whether something you linked does not count because you have not trusted it yet. A circled answer alone does not solve a question.

Some questions rest on others: part of their evidence is that another question is solved. That part fills in by itself when the other question is solved (there is nothing to link: while you are linking to a question, the timeline's line of each question it rests on, and of your circled answer to it, says counted ✓ (solved) or counts once this question is solved), and the question lists what it rests on and which of those are not solved yet. Where papers are earned, the notice also says what solving the question brought: a paper, with a button to open it, or nothing new; and the paper that answers the question, where there is one.

8. Observations: linking the evidence

You point out that something on the timeline bears on an open question: open the event and choose the question under Relevant to…, or press Link observations… on a question and go down the timeline, where each entry gets a + relevant button; Done linking, in the bar at the top of the timeline, ends it (pressing Link observations… on another question moves the linking to that one). If the event tells something about that question, what it shows is written under the question, with where it came from, for example:

? Who took the inkstand?
Shows: the housekeeper was at the market all morning ¶3

A link is checked as you make it. If the event is evidence for the question, the link is kept, and it stays: you cannot take it back, and Undo does not. If it is not, the link is refused with Tried: it shows nothing about this question (for an entry that is only that someone spoke, the note adds that what they said are entries of their own, each of which can be linked), and from then on that question's button on that event reads ✗ tried (on the timeline, tried ✗) and is off: the page remembers what you have tried, and Undo does not clear it. Under each open question, not tried yet: 31 of 40 entries counts what is left for it, and while you are linking, only what is not tried yet hides the entries already linked to the question or tried for it. So you can always work a question through by going down what is left; reasoning gets there sooner.

What a link shows stays only as long as the event is trusted, as likely or confirmed. One event can be evidence for more than one question, and what it shows may differ for each: link it to every question it bears on. A statement you no longer believe can be evidence too: what someone said falsely can show something about them. Distrust the statement (its own trust, in its detail: unlikely or ruled out) and link it; what it shows is filled in only while you disbelieve it. Some evidence needs something else before it shows anything: a second event linked too, a statement disbelieved, an event trusted as likely or confirmed, another question's answer circled, a reasoning move made from it (the entry the move gives is then the one to link), or, for a thing somebody said, the whole statement it is part of, disbelieved and linked. Such a link is kept, its detail says it is part of something not yet complete, and the question lists it so, until the rest is there; it does not say what the rest is. The gate's notice counts such an observation too, while it waits: 2 of 3, and 1 more begun. Some answers are not a name but a description you build this way.

Relevant to… lists the open questions in the order of the Questions list. Put them in the order you like there: drag a question by its ≡ (or focus it and use the arrow keys), or press ↑ to send it to the top. A dividing line in that list decides what is offered: drag it up and only the questions above it appear, with Show the others for the rest. A question newly raised comes in just above the line.

9. Reasoning: the moves

An event marked ⚙ on the timeline is one you can reason from. Open it, and its detail offers the moves that fit it. Each move puts a new entry on the timeline, marked as your reasoning, trusted as far as its weakest premise. The page checks every move against the timeline, and offers only those that hold.

It changed
On a state (where something is, who has it, whether a door is open): a state lasts until something changes it. If the timeline has two different states of the same thing, It changed points out that something changed it between them, and that becomes a question of its own: what did?
Something is missing here
On a rule that something happens at set times (a bell rung every hour, a ledger written up each evening), or on the record of it: when the record on the timeline has every time but one, Something is missing here points out that nothing happened at that one. While a paper that holds part of the record is only half read, the button waits: read the paper to its end first. The entry is your own, and it rests on the rule and on the record: if you distrust either, or the timeline comes to show it happened after all, it no longer holds.
What it needs and what it does
On an action: Point out what it needs and what it does lists what had to be so just before it (to take something, you must be where it is) and what is so just after, each with what the timeline says about it.
What is this for?
In a story that tells of someone's intentions, on an action or an intention: the intentions on the timeline it could serve, stated in the story or circled by you. For this links them; the page checks that what it brings about meets the goal, or a need of a step already linked.
What would this need?
On a plan, one the story describes or one you have built by linking steps to an intention: each step's needs, met by an earlier step, by the timeline, or by nothing, and whether the steps reach the goal. See Plans.

Your reasoning rests on what it was drawn from. If that changes (you distrust a premise, or change a circle), the move stops holding: a notice in the corner says so, and the entry's detail lists it under No longer fits. It comes back if what changed changes back. Withdraw takes a move back for good.

10. The room

Some stories turn on how a place is arranged. Those have a Room panel; the others do not.

  • The ways in. A row for each way into a closed place, as the story tells of it. Mark each one closed or open, separately for a person and for something small. The page refuses a mark nothing on the timeline supports.
  • Something got through. If something crossed between two places, there is a way between them, even one nobody has found.
  • A route. Build a way from one place to another, a link at a time, from what is on the timeline: from where something could have started to where it had to reach. The finished route goes on the timeline as an entry of its own; link it to the question like any other, and what it shows (what could have come that way) is filled in.

11. Plans and their checks

A story that tells of someone's plans offers more. Once a plan's needs are open (What would this need?), its detail offers three checks:

  • What stopped it? If an intended step never happened, what on the timeline prevented it.
  • Compare with another plan: two plans side by side, step by step, with their goals and whether each is reached.
  • Does it account for this? Whether the plan explains something else on the timeline.

A plan's needs that nothing meets are listed in red. That is often where the next question lies.

12. Cards and the story's names

Every name in the story is a link: a person, an animal, a place, a thing. Click one for its card in the detail: the questions about it, the sentences of the story that mention it, and its own timeline (only the events it takes part in).

The Cards list holds everyone and everything met so far, by kind (people, animals, places, things…), each a link to its card.

13. Hints

Hint says what a careful reader would do next, from wherever you are. If one of your marks is wrong where it matters, or you distrust something a careful reader would believe, the hint starts there. Otherwise it points to the next step: a question to answer before the story gives it away, an observation to link, a move to make, or simply "read on".

Each press goes a step further: first where to look (the thing it names is a link: click it to select it, or to open the paper it tells you to read), then the reasoning, then the move itself, with Do it to make it for you. After each press the button waits a little (30 seconds unless you change it in Settings), so that you think before you ask again.

14. Undo

Undo takes back your last change of trust, of a mark, or of your reasoning, one at a time. Reading on is not undone.

15. Watching the autoplay

Watch the autoplay… in Settings shows a careful reader playing the case through the page, from the first paragraph to the last: every circle, every trust, every link and move, with a caption saying what each one is and why. It contains every answer, so watch it only once you have played, or if you do not mind spoilers.

Your own timeline is kept aside and comes back when you stop. A strip of controls goes with it: it starts paused: choose a speed, then Start; then Pause / Resume, Step (the next move or paragraph, then pause), the speed, from ¼× to 4×, and Stop. At normal speed a short case takes about a minute.

16. Settings and starting over

⚙ Settings has two parts. Clicking anywhere outside it closes it.

The timeline

Hide what is ruled out
Take what you have ruled out off the timeline (off by default: it stays, struck through).
Clashes with what is uncertain
Whether two statements that cannot both be so are listed, in grey, while one of them is still uncertain (on by default), or only once you trust both (see Contradictions).
Trust in … as narrator
One for each narrator of the case: how far you trust what they tell you. Lowering it lowers everything that came through them; by default it is confirmed.

The page

Layout
Three columns, Two columns, with tabs or One column, with tabs. A phone always shows one.
Theme
As the system, Light or Dark. This guide follows the same choice.
Wait between hints
None, 10 or 30 seconds, or a minute.
To read past an answer
What the gate asks of a question that has evidence to link: show it (the evidence linked and the right answer circled; the default), or commit to any answer. A question is solved only by its evidence and the right answer, whichever you choose.
Links
Checked as you make them (the default): evidence is kept and cannot be removed, and a link that shows nothing is refused and marked as tried (see observations). Or all kept, as evidence or as notes: nothing is refused or marked, a link that shows nothing stays under the question as your own note, and any link can be removed by clicking it again. Changing back to checked turns such notes into tried marks.
Answers
Marked from the evidence (the default, in the cases that have it written; a question without it is marked by you): you do not mark answers at all. Or marked by you: you circle and cross out the answers yourself. With the answers marked from the evidence: Each thing you link shows a statement; when statements you have shown imply something together, the page says what follows, under the question, over the statements it came from. An answer is crossed out when what you have shown rules it out, and circled when it follows, and the question is solved the moment its answer follows: not every piece of evidence a question has is needed for that. Distrust something an implication rests on and it is taken back. What follows takes part in the contradictions like anything else on your timeline: if it cannot stand with a statement you still believe, the two are listed against each other, and tracing it shows the evidence it follows from. Where what follows says that something was so at a time (someone was not in a place, a round was not made), it is put on the timeline as an entry of your reasoning. Not every step is certain: where an answer rests on a step that is only probable, or on a statement you hold only as likely, it is marked (likely), with a dashed outline. A likely answer still settles its question. What is only likely contradicts nothing; if it cannot stand with something you believe, the two are listed as unlikely together.
What is left to try
Listed (the default) or not listed: the Left to try box above the questions.
Seed of this game
Where papers are earned: the number that decided which question brings which paper, and replay it, an address that starts a new game with the same papers in the same places (it asks before clearing a game in hand).

Saving and starting over

Each case saves itself in this browser as you play: close the page and come back, and you are where you left off. Reset trust… takes back every level you have set (each narrator goes back to confirmed) and keeps the rest. Start over… asks first, then clears everything (your place in the story, your trust, marks and reasoning) and starts again from the first paragraph (where papers are earned, with a new seed). It keeps the layout, the theme, the wait between hints and the gate's rule.

Settings also links to this guide, to All the cases… (the start page) and to the earlier game. Hover over almost any button or setting for a short explanation.

17. The full cases and the earlier game

The page was first built on two Sherlock Holmes stories by Arthur Conan Doyle (1892), narrated by Dr Watson: Silver Blaze and The Adventure of the Speckled Band. They are long, and experimental, and kept as they stood when the short cases began, with their own copy of the page and its own guide.

Before that came an earlier game on Silver Blaze, in which you collect cards and build inferences to beat Holmes to his deductions. It is kept as it was, with its own guide: the earlier game. Each keeps its own saves.
